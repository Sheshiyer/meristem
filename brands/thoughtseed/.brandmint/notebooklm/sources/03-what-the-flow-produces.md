*Outcomes, deliverables, and inheritance*

## The promise

One coherent system from requirement to handoff.

Not a deck. Not a prototype. Not a strategic narrative. A system.

A system the founder's team can run, extend, and own without depending on the people who built it.

## What enters the loop

A founder brings a requirement.

The requirement is usually:
- Too entangled to split across vendors
- Important enough to matter when it ships and when it doesn't
- Cross-disciplinary — touching product, interface, behavior, engineering, and operations at once
- Operating under real constraints — timelines, existing systems, team capacity

The messy version is fine. Half-formed is welcome. The loop's job is to take the messy version and hand back a coherent system.

## What the loop does to the requirement

The loop moves the requirement through four stages, each feeding the next.

**Framing** — surfaces the real decision pressure, the tradeoffs, and the people who will use the system. The founder sees the real problem before anything is built.

**System design** — designs the interface and the operating logic together. User behavior is treated as part of the system. Adoption logic and build reality stay aligned.

**Build** — senior engineers ship what was framed. Decisions stay close to the work. What was framed is what ships.

**Handoff** — documentation, training, and control surfaces ship with the build. The founder inherits a system they can run, extend, and own.

## What the founder inherits

When the loop ends, the founder holds:

| Inherits | Description |
|----------|-------------|
| A running system | Production code, not a deck or a slide |
| Documentation | Written to be read by the team that will run it |
| Control surfaces | Operational artifacts that keep the system legible |
| A model of the problem | They can carry the forward without us |

The founder does not inherit:
- Dependency on the original builder
- A system that works on launch day and breaks at ownership transfer
- A beautiful deck disconnected from shipped reality
- An always-on retainer obligation

## What changes for the founder

### Before the loop

The founder was:
- The full-time translator between five vendors
- Holding the only model of how requirement, user, product, and operations connect
- Carrying the burden of coherence because no one else could
- Anxious that the polished pitch might hide shallow delivery
- Stuck with a system that works on launch day and breaks at ownership transfer

### After the loop

The founder is:
- Free of the translator role — one team held the whole problem
- Confident that what was framed is what shipped
- Proud of a coherent, well-crafted system that reflects real taste and rigor
- The owner of a system her team can run and extend
- Trusted by her team because the artifacts proved the loop is real

The shift is not in the founder's beliefs or market position. The shift is in the work itself — what they hold in their hands, what their team can do without them, and whether the artifacts around the system support ongoing operation.

## What the founder does next

After the loop ends, the founder can:
- Run the system internally
- Extend it with their own team
- Onboard new operators using the documentation
- Make decisions against the model of the problem they carry
- Choose to come back for a new engagement when the next entangled requirement appears

The loop does not require the founder to return. The loop does not lock the founder in. The loop ends with ownership, and ownership is the goal.

## Service modules under one operating logic

The loop runs three service modules, but they are not independent. They are engaged together, not stitched from separate teams.

### Requirement Architecture

Frame the requirement before building it. Problem framing, behavior mapping, operating constraints, and decision logic — settled before implementation.

### Product & Interface Systems

Build the system so it gets used. Interface and operating logic designed together. Adoption logic and build reality stay aligned.

### Handoff & Decision Systems

Hand the system back so it can be run. Documentation, training, control surfaces, and operational artifacts. Handoff is a first-class output.

## Engagement models

### System Sprint

A short, high-leverage framing or prototype cycle that clarifies the requirement and de-risks the next build decision.

### Requirement-to-Handoff Build

Scoped delivery from framing through implementation and ownership transfer. Fewer projects, deeper integration, a real handoff at the end.

## What the loop is not for

The loop does not run:
- Single-slice work — a logo, a pitch deck, a one-off prototype that does not connect to a system
- Always-on retainers — the loop has an end, and the end is ownership transfer
- Marketing or growth campaigns disconnected from product reality
- Decorative identity work without a system behind it

The loop is for problems that cannot be split. The loop is for founders who need a coherent system, not a stack of disconnected deliverables.

## What the loop asks of the founder

To run well, the loop needs:
- Access to the people who will use the system
- A real window into how the founder wants to run and own the result
- Tolerance for the requirement being reframed as the work proceeds
- Willingness to engage with the operators doing the work, not an account layer

The loop does not need:
- A polished brief before starting
- A pre-existing model of the problem
- Decisional authority for every tradeoff (the founder delegates within the loop)

## The closing thought

The loop is not a methodology. The loop is what we do. The loop produces inheritable systems. The loop ends with the founder running the system without us. The loop is the studio, and the studio is the loop. Every engagement runs the same motion.
